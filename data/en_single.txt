Natural language processing (NLP) has changed rapidly in the last decade.
Early NLP systems relied on hand-crafted features and shallow classifiers
such as support vector machines. Support vector machines were strong
baselines for text classification, but they could not capture long-range
context. SVMs remain competitive on small datasets, and SVMs need little
tuning compared with deep models. The introduction of word embeddings made
distributed semantics practical, and word embeddings remain a standard
input representation.

Modern natural language processing is dominated by neural networks. A
neural network trained with gradient descent can learn features directly
from raw text, and a second neural network can refine them. Deep neural
networks with many layers outperform shallow models on most benchmarks.
The Transformer architecture replaced recurrent designs in almost every
NLP task, and Transformer models now define the state of the art. BERT,
short for Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT),
showed that pre-training a deep bidirectional encoder transfers well to
downstream tasks. Large language models extend this idea: large language
models are pre-trained on massive corpora and then adapted by fine-tuning
or prompting.

Machine learning pipelines for NLP still depend on careful data
preprocessing. Tokenization, normalization, and data preprocessing choices
strongly affect downstream accuracy. Evaluation of machine learning models
uses held-out test sets, and cross validation remains common when labeled
data is scarce.
